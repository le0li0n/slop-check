# Platinum Card Finally Gets Me

For eleven years I have been carrying a piece of metal in my wallet that did not know who I was.

That is a strange thing to say about a credit card, but any strategist who has sat through a segmentation workshop will recognise the feeling. Amex knew my spend. It knew my categories, my velocity, my average ticket. What it did not know was anything useful about me. So it kept sending offers for helicopter transfers to the Hamptons, a place I have never been and have no plans to visit, and steakhouse credits in cities I pass through once a decade.

Then, last month, something changed. The offers started landing. A statement credit at the specific airline I actually fly. A hotel partner in the two cities where I have recurring client work. And — this is the one that got me — a dining credit at a restaurant three streets from my office, in the week I had four dinners booked. Nothing about this was magic. All of it was data they had held for a decade. What changed was that they finally did something with it.

I want to pull on that thread, because there is a lesson in it that has very little to do with credit cards.

## The gap between having data and using it

Most large organisations are not data-poor. They are activation-poor. The gap is not between what they know and what they could know — it is between what they know and what they are structurally able to act on.

Amex has always had my transaction history. What it lacked was the operational plumbing to turn "this customer flies BA out of Heathrow eleven times a year" into an offer that reflects that fact, at a cost per offer low enough to justify making it. The constraint was never insight. It was the marginal cost of personalisation, plus the organisational friction of getting a partner deal, a creative treatment, and a targeting rule through three departments for a segment of one.

That marginal cost has collapsed. That is the actual story. And it has collapsed across an enormous range of businesses simultaneously, which means a lot of strategy documents written on the assumption of expensive personalisation are now describing a world that no longer exists.

## Three things this should prompt you to check

**Where are you still averaging?** Every place your business currently serves a segment rather than a customer is a place where the economics may have just shifted underneath you. Pricing tiers. Service levels. Onboarding flows. Renewal conversations. If the reason for the average was cost rather than principle, revisit it.

**What does your organisation structurally prevent?** In most firms, the binding constraint on personalisation is not technology — it is that eleven people have to approve a variant. If the cost of producing a thousand variants has fallen to near zero but your approval process still assumes six, you have not captured the gain. You have simply moved the bottleneck somewhere less visible.

**Are you confusing relevance with intimacy?** This is the trap. The Amex offers landed because they were *useful*, not because they were intimate. Nothing in them implied surveillance. There is a version of the same capability that feels invasive and destroys trust, and the line between the two is thinner than most marketing teams believe. Relevance earns permission. Overreach spends it.

## The uncomfortable part

The strategic question is not "should we personalise more." Everyone will. The question is what happens to differentiation when everyone can do this.

If relevance becomes table stakes rather than an advantage, then the durable position is not in the targeting — it is in having something worth targeting with. Amex's offers work because Amex has partnerships worth redeeming. The capability is commoditising. The inventory behind it is not.

Worth asking which one you actually own.
