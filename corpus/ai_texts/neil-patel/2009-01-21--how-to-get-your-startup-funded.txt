# How to Get Your Startup Funded

Somewhere in the last decade, "raise a round" became shorthand for "succeed." It shouldn't have. Most companies that get funded don't need to be, and plenty that need capital never get it because they misunderstand what they're actually being asked for.

If you work in marketing or content, you have a specific and underrated advantage in this process — and a specific blind spot. The advantage: you already know how to make an argument land with a skeptical audience. The blind spot: you may assume that's the whole job. It isn't. But it's more of the job than most founders realize.

Here's what actually moves a fundraise.

## Start by deciding whether you should raise at all

Venture capital is not a prize. It's a particular financial instrument with a particular shape, and that shape only fits a particular kind of business.

A VC fund needs a small number of investments to return the entire fund. That math means they're not looking for a company that will be reliably profitable at $8M in revenue. They're looking for the one that could plausibly be worth a billion dollars. If your business is a great agency, a strong SaaS tool for a niche market, or a content brand with real margins and modest ceiling — venture money will actively distort it. You'll be pushed to grow faster than the business wants to, and the terms you signed will make a good outcome feel like a failure.

The alternatives are real. Revenue-based financing, small business loans, angel money on friendlier terms, or simply customers paying you — these fund plenty of companies that never appear on a TechCrunch funding roundup. Ask what the money is *for* before you ask who might give it to you. If the honest answer is "to feel legitimate," don't raise.

## Understand what an investor is actually buying

An investor is buying a claim on a future that doesn't exist yet. Everything in a pitch is an attempt to make that future feel less speculative.

They are trying to resolve four questions, roughly in this order:

**Is this a big enough market?** Not "is this a good idea" — is the addressable opportunity large enough that a huge outcome is even geometrically possible? A brilliant product in a $40M market is a bad venture investment no matter how brilliant it is.

**Why now?** What changed in the world that makes this possible or necessary today when it wasn't three years ago? A regulatory shift, a cost curve, a behavior change, a new platform. If nothing changed, the obvious question is why no one has already done this.

**Why you?** What do you know, or have, that a competent competitor doesn't? Domain experience, a distribution channel, a technical edge, an unusual insight into the customer.

**What proves it?** Evidence. Whatever form it takes at your stage.

Most decks answer question one at length, gesture at three, and skip two and four. Reverse that emphasis and you'll be in the top decile of pitches an investor sees this month.

## Evidence is the whole game, and it's stage-dependent

The single most common failure is bringing the wrong kind of evidence for the stage you're at.

At pre-seed, you likely have no revenue and that's fine. Your evidence is the quality of your thinking and any signal that customers want this: waitlist conversion, letters of intent, a prototype people use unprompted, an unusually specific understanding of a problem you've lived. Investors at this stage are underwriting you, not the numbers.

At seed, they want early traction with a *shape* to it. Not just "we have 400 users" but "we have 400 users, 60% are weekly active after eight weeks, and they came almost entirely from one channel we can spend into." Retention beats growth. A leaky bucket that's filling fast is still a leaky bucket, and experienced investors will find the hole in about four minutes.

At Series A, the bar shifts from promise to machine. You need to show that you put a dollar in one end and get more than a dollar out the other, repeatably. Payback period, net revenue retention, channel concentration, gross margin. This is where a lot of well-marketed companies come apart — the story is great, the unit economics are not, and no amount of narrative closes that gap.

Know which stage's evidence you're being judged against. Bringing Series A metrics to a pre-seed conversation is fine. Bringing pre-seed vibes to a Series A meeting is fatal.

## Where your professional skills genuinely transfer

Now the part where your background helps.

**Narrative structure is a real differentiator.** Most technical founders pitch like a feature list. You know how to build a story with a premise, a tension, and a resolution — and how to sequence information so each slide earns the next. A deck is a piece of persuasive content with a very specific conversion goal. Treat it like one.

**You understand audience segmentation.** Not all investors want the same thing. A seed fund with a consumer thesis and a growth fund with an enterprise focus are different audiences requiring different emphasis on the same facts. Research the fund's portfolio, thesis, and check size before the meeting. Sending an identical deck to sixty funds is the fundraising equivalent of an untargeted blast, and it performs about as well.

**You know distribution is a strategy, not a line item.** A great many pitches treat go-to-market as an afterthought — "we'll do content and paid social." If you can articulate a credible, specific, defensible acquisition strategy with real numbers behind it, you will stand out sharply. This is arguably the most valuable thing you bring.

**You can tell the difference between clarity and polish.** Beautiful decks with nothing in them are common. Investors are pattern-matching on substance, and over-designed slides can read as compensation. Clear beats pretty.

## The mechanics that actually determine outcomes

**Warm introductions dominate.** Cold outreach works occasionally; introductions work far more reliably. Map who in your network touches the funds you want, and ask for specific intros rather than general help. Give the person introducing you a short, forwardable paragraph — you're writing their email for them.

**Run it as a compressed process.** Fundraising over six months, one meeting at a time, is a slow death. Batch your outreach so first meetings happen in the same two-week window. Parallel conversations create genuine urgency; sequential ones create the impression that you've been rejected by everyone earlier in the list.

**"No" is the default and it isn't about you.** Investors pass on the vast majority of what they see, including things that later work brilliantly. Ask for the specific reason, note what recurs, and adjust. Ten passes citing the same concern is data.

**Read the terms, not just the number.** Valuation is the headline; liquidation preferences, board composition, pro-rata rights, and option pool sizing determine what actually happens to you. Get a lawyer who does this regularly. This is not the place to save money.

**Diligence is a real phase.** Have your metrics reconciled, your cap table clean, and your customer references warned. Surprises found in diligence kill deals that had already been verbally agreed.

## The unglamorous truth

The strongest fundraising position is not needing to raise. Companies with revenue, a clear path to more, and eighteen months of runway get better terms and faster yeses than companies three weeks from missing payroll — and the gap isn't close.

Which means the most effective fundraising work often isn't fundraising at all. It's getting to a place where the story tells itself, because the numbers are doing the talking and you're just providing context. Great narrative accelerates a real business. It cannot substitute for one, and the people writing checks have seen enough attempts to know the difference on sight.
