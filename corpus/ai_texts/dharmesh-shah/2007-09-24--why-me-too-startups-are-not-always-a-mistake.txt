# Why "Me Too" Startups Are Not Always A Mistake

There's a reflex in venture that fires before the analysis does. A founder walks in, describes their product, and someone at the table says: "So how is this different from [incumbent]?" The question is usually rhetorical. It means: *you're late, and you don't have a reason to exist.*

Sometimes that's right. Often it isn't. The reflex confuses two very different failure modes — being derivative, and being redundant — and in doing so it filters out a category of company that has produced some of the best returns in the asset class.

## The obvious point, briefly

Google was the thirteenth search engine. Facebook launched into a market with Friendster and MySpace already at scale. Slack entered a category that had HipChat, Campfire, and Yammer in it. Zoom was founded in 2011, when WebEx, GoToMeeting and Skype were mature products with enterprise contracts.

Everyone knows these examples, which is exactly why they've stopped functioning as arguments. They get filed under "execution matters" and then ignored the next time a founder without a novel category walks into the room. Worth asking what was actually true about those markets rather than restating the anecdote.

## Three conditions that make a late entrant rational

**The incumbent's constraint is structural, not intellectual.** The interesting question is never "can the incumbent see the problem?" It's "can they act on it?" WebEx knew its product was bad. It couldn't fix it, because the codebase was old, the customers were enterprise, and the revenue was tied to an install base that punished rewrites. A late entrant that is faster, simpler, or differently-priced isn't out-thinking the incumbent — it's exploiting a commitment the incumbent can't reverse. Channel conflict, an existing pricing model, a large services organization, a public-market margin profile: these are more durable moats *against* the incumbent than most startups' moats are for them.

**The market has been validated but not saturated.** A category with several mediocre players and visible customer frustration is a different proposition from an empty one. Someone else has already paid to educate the buyer, establish the budget line, and prove people will pay. The remaining question is share, not existence — a much cheaper question to answer. First movers frequently absorb the cost of market creation and then lose the market. That's not an anomaly, it's the base rate in categories where the underlying technology is still moving.

**A platform shift has reset the substrate.** Every meaningful change in the underlying technology — cloud, mobile, and now the current model generation — makes a set of solved problems unsolved again. A company built on the old substrate carries its architecture as debt. "Salesforce for X, but rebuilt on Y" is not a lack of imagination; it's often the whole thesis. The differentiation lives in the architecture, which doesn't demo well and doesn't fit in a one-line pitch, but it is real and it is defensible for as long as the incumbent's rewrite would take.

## What actually separates the good ones from the bad ones

The distinction that matters isn't novel versus derivative. It's whether the founder has a *specific, falsifiable* account of why the incumbents lose from here.

Bad answers: "we'll have better UX," "we'll execute harder," "the market is big enough for several players," "the incumbents are slow." These are wishes. They're unfalsifiable, and they don't survive contact with a competitor who takes the threat seriously.

Good answers name the mechanism. *They can't cut price without breaking their margin story to public investors. They can't sell direct without destroying their partner channel. Their data model can't support multi-tenancy, and rewriting it takes three years. Their best engineers left in 2024.* These are testable claims. You can check them, and if they're true, the founder has found a real structural asymmetry rather than an aspiration.

The second thing worth checking is whether the founder chose the market or defaulted into it. Late entrants who did serious work — who can tell you exactly which segment the incumbent underserves and why, who have talked to the churned customers rather than the happy ones — are running a strategy. Founders who picked a crowded market because it looked easy to explain to investors are running a slogan.

## The cost of the reflex

Novelty is the easiest thing to underwrite and one of the weakest predictors of outcome. A genuinely new category means no comparables, unproven demand, an unbuilt budget line, and a buyer who doesn't know they have the problem. Plenty of venture returns come from exactly that. But the reflexive dismissal of the late entrant isn't rigor — it's a heuristic that's cheap to apply and rarely audited.

The better filter is a harder question: what has to be true about the incumbent for this company to win, and is it true? That question sorts the good "me too" companies from the bad ones far more reliably than asking whether the idea is new.
