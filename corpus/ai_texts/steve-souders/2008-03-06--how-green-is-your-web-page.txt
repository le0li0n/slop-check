How Green Is Your Web Page?

Every request your server answers burns electricity somewhere: in the data center, across the network, on the user's device. Most of us never measure it, because nothing in our tooling asks us to. Lighthouse scores load time. It doesn't score carbon.

That's starting to change, and it's worth understanding why, because the fixes overlap heavily with the performance work you're already doing.

**The rough math.** A page's carbon footprint breaks down into three buckets: data center energy (how the servers and network gear are powered), network transfer (how many bytes cross the wire, and over what infrastructure), and end-user device energy (what it costs to parse, render, and run your JavaScript). The Sustainable Web Design model, used by tools like Website Carbon and Ecograder, estimates grams of CO2 per page view from these factors. It's an estimate, not a lab measurement, but it's directionally useful, and directionally is enough to act on.

**Bytes are the lever you control most directly.** Transfer size correlates with energy use because every byte has to be generated, compressed, transmitted, and rendered. This means the standard performance playbook — image compression and modern formats (AVIF, WebP), removing unused JavaScript, code-splitting, deferring non-critical CSS, caching aggressively, minimizing third-party scripts — is also the carbon playbook. If you've already done a performance audit this year, you've already done most of the carbon audit too. The overlap is not a coincidence: both are measuring waste.

**Where it diverges from pure performance:** hosting. A CDN edge cache and a data center running on renewable energy can cut emissions substantially even for an unoptimized page. Green Web Foundation maintains a lookup for hosting providers with verified renewable commitments; it's worth five minutes to check where your infrastructure actually sits. Also consider request count independent of size — DNS lookups, TLS handshakes, and round trips all cost energy even when payloads are small, which is an argument for HTTP/2 or /3 and for consolidating third-party origins rather than scattering requests across a dozen ad-tech and analytics domains.

**Practical starting points, roughly in order of effort-to-impact:**

- Run your homepage and top three landing pages through Website Carbon or Ecograder to get a baseline. Treat the number as relative, not absolute.
- Audit third-party scripts. Ad networks, chat widgets, and analytics tags are frequently the largest uncounted weight on a page, and the easiest to cut without touching product code.
- Check your host against the Green Web Foundation database, or ask your infra team what your cloud provider discloses about the regions you deploy to.
- Set an image budget and enforce it in CI, the same way you'd enforce a bundle-size budget.
- Cache aggressively and set long TTLs for static assets — fewer regenerated bytes, fewer requests.

None of this requires a new discipline or a new dashboard vendor. It requires treating "how much does this page cost to serve and render, repeated across millions of views" as a number worth knowing, the same way you already treat load time. The infrastructure for measuring it is free and takes minutes to run. The harder part, as usual, is deciding it matters enough to look.
